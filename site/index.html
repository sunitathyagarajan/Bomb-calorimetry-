A. What a bomb calorimeter measures

The first law says that the change in internal energy of a system is the heat it absorbs plus the work done on it: ΔU = q + w. For pressure–volume work, w = −∫PextdV. The bomb is a rigid, sealed steel vessel, so dV = 0 and no PV work is done:

ΔU = qV(constant volume)

The heat released by the reaction goes into the bomb, the bucket water and the hardware around them. Together they behave like one object with a heat capacity C, called the energy equivalent of the calorimeter:

qcal = C·ΔT = −qV,rxn = −ΔUrxn(1)

Why not just burn the sample in open air at constant pressure? Combustion needs a large excess of oxygen and must go to completion quickly. A sealed, oxygen-charged vessel does both, keeps hot gases from carrying heat away, and contains the products so nothing escapes the measurement.

C. From ΔU to ΔH

Tables report enthalpies, so you must convert. Since H = U + PV and the gases behave nearly ideally, at constant temperature:

ΔcH = ΔcU + Δ(PV) ≈ ΔcU + Δngas·RT(2)

Here Δngas = (moles of gaseous products) − (moles of gaseous reactants). Liquids and solids contribute almost nothing to Δ(PV). Use the explorer to see how large this correction is.

D. Calibrating with benzoic acid

You cannot compute C from first principles because the bomb, bucket, water and stirrer all absorb part of the heat, in amounts that are hard to calculate. Instead you burn a known mass of benzoic acid, a standard with an accurately known specific energy of combustion (course value: Δcu = −26.41 kJ g⁻¹; see the NIST SRM 39j certificate). The fuse wire also burns and releases heat, so you correct for it:

C = [ mBA·|ΔcuBA| + mwire·|Δcuwire| ] / ΔT(3)

Here |Δcuwire| = 1400 cal g⁻¹ = 5.857 kJ g⁻¹ for the Parr fuse wire. mwire is the mass of wire that actually burned, which is the initial mass minus the unburned pieces you recover.

E. Hess's law and the resonance energy of benzene

1,5,9-trans,trans,cis-cyclododecatriene (CCDT, C₁₂H₁₈) has exactly the same bonds as one "cyclohexatriene" (a benzene with three localized, non-interacting double bonds) plus one cyclohexane: 3 C=C, 9 C–C and 18 C–H. If benzene had no resonance stabilization, burning gaseous CCDT would release the same heat as burning gaseous benzene and gaseous cyclohexane together. Real benzene sits lower in energy, so burning it releases less heat. The difference is the resonance energy.

Step through the thermodynamic cycle

practice
ΔcH(X, g) = ΔcH(X, l) − ΔvapH(X)(4)
Eres = ΔcH(C₆H₆, g) + ΔcH(C₆H₁₂, g) − ΔcH(C₁₂H₁₈, g)(5)

Trouton's rule estimates an unknown enthalpy of vaporization from the normal boiling point, because the entropy of vaporization of many non-polar liquids is close to 85–88 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: ΔvapH ≈ (85 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹)·Tb. Use this for CCDT, which boils at about 231 °C.

Introduction

Heats of formation of organic compounds are usually found from measured heats of combustion. In this experiment you measure the heats of combustion of a solid (naphthalene) and a liquid (1,5,9-trans,trans,cis-cyclododecatriene, CCDT) in a bomb calorimeter. The principle is described in Experiment 6 of Garland, Nibler & Shoemaker.

You then use the heat of combustion of CCDT to estimate the resonance stabilization energy of benzene, the energy difference between benzene and the hypothetical molecule cyclohexatriene. With no resonance stabilization, the enthalpy of combustion for

C₁₂H₁₈(g) + 33/2 O₂(g) → 12 CO₂(g) + 9 H₂O(l)(1)

would equal the sum of the combustion enthalpies of one mole of gaseous benzene (C₆H₆) and one mole of gaseous cyclohexane (C₆H₁₂). Think about why before you come to lab. The ΔcH° values for liquid benzene and cyclohexane, and their heats of vaporization, are in standard references such as the NIST Chemistry WebBook. You will measure ΔcU for liquid CCDT and correct it to the gas phase with a heat of vaporization estimated from Trouton's rule.

Safety

High-pressure oxygen and combustion.
  • Wear safety glasses at all times. Keep oil, grease and lotions away from oxygen fittings; they can ignite in pure O₂.
  • The total charge must not release more than 1200 cal (5.02 kJ). Check your mass with the charge check below before pressing a pellet.
  • Never fire a bomb that leaks. If bubbles rise from the bomb in the bucket, stop and tell your TA.
  • Stand clear of the calorimeter while firing. Don't touch the ignition leads.
  • After a run, release the pressure slowly through the valve before you unscrew the cap.
  • Handle naphthalene and CCDT with gloves and read their Safety Data Sheets (SDS) before lab. Dispose of residues as your TA directs.

Reading a thermogram

A typical run gives a temperature–time curve like the one below. Each run has three regimes:

  1. Fore period. Temperature is nearly constant and drifts slowly, from stirring and heat exchange with the jacket.
  2. Rise. Temperature rises rapidly right after ignition as heat flows from the bomb into the water.
  3. After period. Temperature falls slowly but steadily as the warmer bucket loses heat to the jacket.

The adiabatic temperature rise ΔT is the rise you would see if all the heat moved into the water instantly, with no heat exchange. To find it, fit straight lines to regimes 1 and 3 and extrapolate both to a common time inside the rise. Parr uses the time at which the temperature reaches 60 % of the total rise. ΔT is the vertical gap between the two lines at that time. The post-lab analyzer does this with your own data.

Part 4 · ΔfH° of naphthalene 19 pts

C₁₀H₈(s) + 12 O₂(g) → 10 CO₂(g) + 4 H₂O(l)
ΔfH°(C₁₀H₈) = 10·ΔfH°(CO₂,g) + 4·ΔfH°(H₂O,l) − ΔcH°(C₁₀H₈)
Hint on uncertaintyIf you treat the CODATA formation enthalpies of CO₂ and H₂O as exact constants, the absolute uncertainty of ΔfH° equals that of your ΔcH°. The relative uncertainty, however, is enormous. Compare the size of ΔfH° with the size of ΔcH° to see why.

Part 5 · Resonance energy of benzene 30 pts

Look up ΔcH° of the liquids and ΔvapH for benzene and cyclohexane in a credible source such as the NIST Chemistry WebBook or the CRC Handbook, and cite it. Use Trouton's rule for CCDT.

Hint on signsConvert every liquid combustion enthalpy to the gas phase first: ΔcH(g) = ΔcH(l) − ΔvapH. The gas releases more heat because it starts higher in enthalpy. A stabilized benzene should give a positive resonance energy in Eq. (5). If you get a negative number, check your signs.

Uncertainty explorer

The manual asks how the precision of ΔcH(CCDT) compares with the precision of the resonance energy. The resonance energy is a small difference between large numbers, near 7300 kJ mol⁻¹, so errors that look tiny in ΔcH become large in Eres.

Uses the masses and ΔT values from Parts 2–3. The bath-volume term assumes the water contributes most of C. The Trouton term assumes ±3 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ in the Trouton constant.
